# Prior art backs the spike, with sharper edges

The research backs the spike's core architecture on both platforms. On macOS, the right way to present a CPU-rendered frame is two IOSurfaces swapped as `CALayer.contents`, because a fresh CGImage per commit costs 13 to 20 ms in independent measurements. On Linux, small per-strip `GdkMemoryTexture`s handed to `snapshot()` are the right shape, because GTK's GPU renderers cache uploads per texture object and never do partial uploads. Ghostty, kitty, Helix, Zed and softbuffer have each hit the same walls the spike hit, and they mostly chose the same exits. Where the research changes the spike's conclusions, it does so by tightening definitions rather than overturning anything. The 14 ms GTK keystroke figure is mostly frame-clock waiting, not app work. A keystroke-to-glass p95 of 16 ms at 60 Hz is physically unattainable for any vsynced, composited client. The single-binary fork-without-exec launcher is safe on Linux under strict conditions (kitty ships it), but it gives up the 2.8 ms launcher, and it is forbidden on macOS. The font tofu is explained by cosmic-text's fallback only searching faces already loaded. The biggest tension the research turns up is that the standard prior-art answer to slow GTK start-up is to keep a resident single instance, which foo_edit's one-process-per-file model rules out, so cold start has to carry the whole budget. The open decisions (binary count, latency definition, renderer, text stack, GTK floor) all have usable evidence. Two of them, the GSK renderer and cosmic-text versus parley, still need one more measurement before they can be closed with confidence.

A note on epistemics: throughout, sourced facts carry a citation. Statements framed as "the researchers infer" or "this suggests" are reasoning from those sources, not things a source states. Gaps are called out as gaps. A few macOS facts come from Apple SDK headers read locally on the research machine (Xcode SDK 27.2) because Apple's documentation pages rendered client-side and could not be fetched. They are primary Apple sources but carry no URL, so they're cited as "SDK header". One Core Text timing comes from a local probe run by the researchers and is labelled that way.

## macOS: IOSurface contents is the only fast path, and the bundle needs a router

### CGImage as layer contents is intrinsically slow; IOSurface is first-class

The spike's ~12 ms per CGImage commit lines up with independent measurements. softbuffer, the main Rust CPU-presentation crate, still builds a new CGImage over a `CGDataProvider` on every `present()` in 0.4.8 ([softbuffer cg.rs](https://docs.rs/crate/softbuffer/latest/source/src/backends/cg.rs)). In its issue #83 a contributor measured **19.8 ms per present on master (CGImage), 13.0 ms when copying into an IOSurface, and 0.74 ms when rendering directly into an IOSurface**, and a 2025 user reported 18 to 20 ms for softbuffer versus 1 to 2 ms for the wgpu-based `pixels` crate ([softbuffer #83](https://github.com/rust-windowing/softbuffer/issues/83)). A maintainer notes that the CGImage route "still requires CoreGraphics to upload the data", and the IOSurface path is milestoned for v0.5 but not shipped (same source). The third-party `denise-macos` crate documents the same design and the same reasoning: a CGImage is "copied whole on every commit however little of it changed", while an IOSurface is read "in place, no copy". It measured 9.2% versus 2% of a core for a small animated surface ([denise_macos ViewSurface](https://docs.rs/denise-macos/latest/denise_macos/struct.ViewSurface.html)).

Apple's CALayer header names IOSurface as a typical `contents` type alongside CGImage, so this is not a hack (SDK header: QuartzCore/CALayer.h:373-378). The same header says `contentsFormat` is only a hint for backing stores made by `-drawLayerInContext` and "does not affect the interpretation of the `contents' property directly" (SDK header: CALayer.h:430-435). That explains why the spike found the CGImage cost didn't move with format or layer mode: the knob it was turning does nothing for assigned contents. Neither Apple's docs nor any source the researchers found says exactly what happens to a CGImage at commit. The researchers infer that the cost doesn't depend on size because it's a fixed per-commit handoff or texture creation, not copy bandwidth, but that mechanism is unconfirmed. An Instruments trace across the app and WindowServer would settle it.

The production trend points the same way. Ghostty's 2025 renderer rework replaced `CAMetalLayer` with a custom IOSurface layer in a layer-hosting view and reported a slightly smaller memory footprint ([Ghostty PR #7620](https://github.com/ghostty-org/ghostty/pull/7620)). Chromium has long fed IOSurfaces into CALayer trees across processes ([Chromium ca_layer_tree_coordinator.h](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/b42c975183b2103c5c039d850926386f631ffe87/ui/accelerated_widget_mac/ca_layer_tree_coordinator.h)). The practical upshot for crate choice: **using softbuffer as shipped would bring the 12 ms regression back**, so the spike's own IOSurface backend should stay.

### Double buffering has two non-obvious rules

There are two rules the spike's damage-only redraw has to respect. First, assigning the same IOSurface object to `contents` again "tells CoreAnimation nothing", so a single surface leaves the window stuck on its first frame. Swapping between two surfaces forces a change on every present, and the only alternative is a private API ([denise_macos](https://docs.rs/denise-macos/latest/denise_macos/struct.ViewSurface.html)). Second, the buffer you get back is **two frames old, not one**, so damage tracking has to cover two frames of damage (buffer age 2) (same source). If the spike only redraws the rows damaged since the last frame, it will show stale rows unless it accumulates damage across both buffers or copies the delta from the front buffer. That's an inference, but it follows directly from the source.

Apple's `contents` documentation warns that a view may overwrite `contents` on a layer that backs it ([Apple: CALayer.contents](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/quartzcore/calayer/contents.md)). That argues for a layer-hosting view or a dedicated sublayer, which is what Ghostty does. softbuffer wraps `setContents` in a `CATransaction` with actions disabled to suppress the default fade ([softbuffer cg.rs](https://docs.rs/crate/softbuffer/latest/source/src/backends/cg.rs)). For colour, `kIOSurfaceColorSpace` takes a serialised `CGColorSpace` and has been available since macOS 10.6 (SDK header: IOSurface/IOSurfaceRef.h:302-307), and Core Animation colour-matches tagged contents when compositing ([Apple: CAMetalLayer.colorspace](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/quartzcore/cametallayer/colorspace.md)). The researchers recommend tagging surfaces sRGB, since theme colours are authored in sRGB, and letting the compositor match to P3 panels. The spike saw Display P3 tagging run slower with CGImage, which is consistent with an extra colour-matching pass, but nothing confirms that. Two things are still unsourced: whether `IOSurfaceLock` is strictly needed for CPU writes, and whether `kIOSurfaceColorSpace` is honoured for layer contents on every macOS version.

### Display link: macOS 14 API, macOS 15 deprecation

The current SDK marks CVDisplayLink deprecated from **macOS 15.0** and points at `NSView`/`NSWindow`/`NSScreen` `displayLink(target:selector:)` (SDK header: CoreVideo/CVDisplayLink.h:51; confirmed by [QEMU issue 2575](https://gitlab.com/qemu-project/qemu/-/issues/2575)). That settles a disagreement in the notes, where one source said 14. The view-based link fires in sync with whichever display the view is on and is available from macOS 14.0 ([Apple: NSView.displayLink](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/appkit/nsview/displaylink(target:selector:).md)). objc2-app-kit 0.3.2 generates it as a plain `unsafe fn` with no availability gate, while objc2 0.6.5 ships an `available!()` macro ([objc2-app-kit 0.3.2](https://crates.io/crates/objc2-app-kit/0.3.2); [objc2 0.6.5](https://crates.io/crates/objc2/0.6.5)). The researchers infer that calling it on macOS 13 would fail at runtime, so either set a 14.0 floor or gate the call. They also recommend rendering on input and presenting on the next tick, then pausing the link when there's no damage, so idle CPU stays near zero.

### Launch Services gives you one instance, so the bundle needs a router

Launch Services normally reuses a running instance and Finder goes through Launch Services, so double-clicking a second file won't start a second process by default ([cocoa-dev 2011](https://lists.apple.com/archives/cocoa-dev/2011/May/msg00261.html)). Running the bundle's Mach-O directly bypasses Launch Services entirely ([macscripter](https://www.macscripter.net/t/how-does-one-prevent-launching-second-copy-of-an-app/71453)). VS Code's `code` CLI is a shell script in the bundle that execs `Contents/MacOS/<name>` directly ([VS Code code.sh](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/vscode/main/resources/darwin/bin/code.sh)), and Zed symlinks a `cli` binary from inside the bundle into `/usr/local/bin` ([Zed macOS docs](https://zed.dev/docs/macos)). The researchers infer the design for foo_edit: the Launch Services instance gets `application:openURLs:` and acts as a thin router, spawning a sibling process (the bundle's own executable with the path as an argument) for each file. `NSWorkspace.OpenConfiguration.createsNewApplicationInstance` is a possible alternative, but it wasn't sourced. Also unverified: whether a process exec'd directly from the bundle registers with Launch Services in a way that changes which instance receives later Finder opens. That needs testing on a real build.

### Activation is cooperative, so the bench result is expected

Since macOS 14, `activateIgnoringOtherApps:` is on the deprecation path, and `-[NSApplication activate]` is only a request: "The framework also does not guarantee that the app will be activated at all". Cooperative activation expects the active app to call `yieldActivationToApplication:` first (SDK header: AppKit/NSApplication.h:222-254; [Apple: activate()](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/appkit/nsapplication/activate().md)). Wine and Electron both adopted this model ([Wine patch thread](https://list.winehq.org/hyperkitty/list/wine-gitlab@list.winehq.org/thread/K4XSASJC5FYH262CAN453KE4ZHNRCKDX/); [Electron #42187](https://ayakael.net/mirrors/electron/commit/220166419f1591f61712c6a9e93d552f444789c8)). So the spike seeing the window fail to become key under a background bench, while working fine from a user's active terminal, is the documented model at work, not a bug. Where foo_edit's own router launches the child, it should yield to it first (inference). A plain CLI launcher has no NSApplication to yield from, so it relies on the terminal being frontmost.

## GTK4: the source code explains the spike's wins and where the time goes

### Why strip textures beat cairo, read from GTK's own source

Reading GTK main (post 4.24.1) shows that the GPU cache stores uploaded images as per-`GdkTexture` render data, so **an unchanged texture object reused across frames isn't uploaded again, and a new object is uploaded in full** ([gsk/gpu/gskgpucache.c](https://github.com/GNOME/gtk/blob/main/gsk/gpu/gskgpucache.c)). The `update_texture`/`update_region` chain that `GdkMemoryTextureBuilder` (4.16+) sets up is consumed only by the render-node diff, and nothing under `gsk/gpu/` reads it. **There is no partial-upload path** ([gdk/gdktexture.c](https://github.com/GNOME/gtk/blob/main/gdk/gdktexture.c); [gsk/gsktexturenode.c](https://github.com/GNOME/gtk/blob/main/gsk/gsktexturenode.c); [docs.gtk.org MemoryTextureBuilder](https://docs.gtk.org/gdk4/class.MemoryTextureBuilder.html)). That is the mechanism behind the spike's result. A whole-window cairo `DrawingArea` hands GSK a fresh surface every frame, about 15.7 MB at 1900x2068x4. A 128 px strip is about 1 MB, which fits the drop from 13 to 15 ms GSK render to 3 ms (the researchers' arithmetic). A browser project relies on the same cache behaviour ([nordstjernen-browser PR #169](https://github.com/nordstjernen-web/nordstjernen-browser/pull/169)).

Ghostty is the strongest prior art. It dropped `GtkGLArea` because GTK's render lifecycle "lives exclusively on the main UI thread". Its replacement widget builds a `GdkDmabufTexture` per frame, with a `GdkMemoryTexture` fallback for CPU frames, and hands it over in `snapshot()` ([ghostty render_surface.zig](https://github.com/ghostty-org/ghostty/blob/main/src/apprt/gtk/class/render_surface.zig)). The researchers draw three refinements from this. Unchanged strips must stay the **same texture object** from frame to frame or the cache misses. Setting `update_region` is still worth doing, but it only shrinks the repaint damage, never the upload. Smaller strips would cut upload bytes further at the cost of more nodes. Nobody has measured whether small strips land in the new 4.24 atlas allocator, and no GTK maintainer statement endorses tiling. The case for it comes from reading the source.

### Renderer selection: Vulkan is the Wayland default and costs start-up time

GTK switched the default renderer on Wayland to Vulkan in 4.16 (September 2024). Other platforms, X11 included, put GL first. The old GL renderer was dropped in 4.17.4, and `ngl` was renamed `gl` in 4.19.1 ([GTK NEWS](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/gtk/main/NEWS); [gdk/wayland/gdkdisplay-wayland.c](https://github.com/GNOME/gtk/blob/main/gdk/wayland/gdkdisplay-wayland.c)). The selection order in source is: a private display data hook, then `GSK_RENDERER`, then Vulkan if the display prefers it, then GL, then the fallbacks, then cairo. The Vulkan probe runs `gdk_display_prepare_vulkan` and dmabuf initialisation, which is where its time goes ([gsk/gskrenderer.c](https://github.com/GNOME/gtk/blob/main/gsk/gskrenderer.c)). Ghostty sets **`GDK_DISABLE=vulkan` unconditionally before GTK init**, citing GL/Vulkan conflicts and that disabling Vulkan "saves hundreds of milliseconds of initialization time on certain systems". It also strips `GDK_DEBUG`, `GDK_DISABLE` and `GSK_RENDERER` from child environments ([ghostty application.zig](https://github.com/ghostty-org/ghostty/blob/main/src/apprt/gtk/class/application.zig); [surface.zig](https://github.com/ghostty-org/ghostty/blob/main/src/apprt/gtk/class/surface.zig)). That fits the spike's 234 ms (default) versus 213 ms (gl) medians. There is one third-party crash report tied to `GDK_DISABLE=vulkan`, but its cause is unclear ([gnome-extension-ghostty-quick-terminal #5](https://github.com/abn/gnome-extension-ghostty-quick-terminal/issues/5)).

Cairo is still maintained, but the docs call it "the fallback Cairo renderer" ([docs.gtk.org Running GTK apps](https://docs.gtk.org/gtk4/running.html); [GTK NEWS](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/gtk/main/NEWS)). The researchers point out that with cairo every texture node is composited on the CPU and offload to subsurfaces is lost. Nobody has measured cairo's keystroke p95 against GL, so its 66 ms cold-start advantage can't yet be weighed against its per-frame cost. `GSK_RENDERER` is read once and cached in a static, so it must be set before the first renderer is created ([gsk/gskrenderer.c](https://github.com/GNOME/gtk/blob/main/gsk/gskrenderer.c)). In Rust 2024, `set_var` is `unsafe`, so it belongs at the top of `main` before any threads exist (inference).

### Start-up: GTK overhead dominates, and the usual cure is off the table

Ghostty's maintainers say most start-up time is GTK overhead. They report about 500 ms on first launch, about 200 ms for a warm single instance, and 33 ms for `+new-window` into a resident process. Their advice is systemd/D-Bus activation plus single-instance, and measuring "from the user's request until the window accepts input" ([Ghostty discussion #10426](https://github.com/ghostty-org/ghostty/discussions/10426)). That's useful, but it runs straight into foo_edit's one-process-per-file design: **the main way prior art makes GTK feel fast is the one foo_edit has ruled out**, so the 250 ms median has to be met cold. The spike's raw Wayland shell at 68 ms against GTK at 147 to 234 ms matches the maintainers' claim. The remaining levers are mostly the researchers' inferences. Use `G_APPLICATION_NON_UNIQUE`, since there's no instance to find and it skips the D-Bus name round trip. Avoid libadwaita unless it's needed, because it adds a library and a stylesheet and nobody has measured its cost. Link with `--as-needed`. Note that from 4.21 the Wayland backend reads settings through the portal, which is a D-Bus round trip ([GTK NEWS 4.21.0](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/gtk/main/NEWS)). Static linking of GTK4 isn't a supported route because GTK depends on loadable modules (researchers' prior knowledge, unsourced). GTK 4.16.2 avoided initialising GL and Vulkan "in most cases", and that's the only start-up entry in NEWS since 4.14 ([GTK NEWS 4.16.2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/gtk/main/NEWS)). There are no primary numbers for the cost of `gtk_init`, CSS, icon themes or libadwaita in 4.20 and later. A Sysprof capture would supply them.

### Frame clock: where the 14 ms and 48 ms come from

On Wayland, GTK's frame clock won't start a paint while it's "throttled", and throttling is only released by the compositor's `wl_surface.frame` callback. There's no public "paint now" API, and `GDK_DEBUG=no-vsync` is a debug flag ([gdk/gdkframeclockidle.c](https://github.com/GNOME/gtk/blob/main/gdk/gdkframeclockidle.c); [gdk/wayland/gdksurface-wayland.c](https://github.com/GNOME/gtk/blob/main/gdk/wayland/gdksurface-wayland.c)). So a keystroke that lands just after a commit can wait up to about two refresh intervals (about 33 ms at 60 Hz) before it paints. That accounts for the spike's p99 tail of 48 ms better than app work does (inference). GTK reworked the frame clock in 4.23.2 and 4.23.3 "to improve precision and reduce frame drops" and added a frame-time overlay to the inspector in 4.23.4 ([GTK NEWS](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/gtk/main/NEWS)), so 4.24 is the version to measure against. The researchers recommend updating the text model directly in the key handler and calling `queue_draw()` straight away, never from an idle callback or timeout. To tell frame-clock waits apart from main-loop stalls, log `GdkFrameClock` layout/paint timestamps and `GdkFrameTimings` presentation times.

### X11 is deprecated but alive for GTK 4's lifetime

GTK 4.17.4 deprecated the X11 and Broadway backends, both slated for removal in GTK 5 ([GTK NEWS 4.17.4](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/gtk/main/NEWS); [Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/GTK-X11-Now-Deprecated)). The GTK project says X11 stays until GTK 4 reaches end of life ([OSNews](https://www.osnews.com/story/141670/gtk-announces-x11-deprecation-new-android-backend-and-much-more/)), and X11 fixes were still landing in 4.24. "X11 best effort" matches upstream's stance closely. Since 4.19.2, input methods on Wayland go through text-input, with `GTK_IM_MODULE=simple` as the fallback for compose and dead keys ([GTK NEWS 4.19.2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/gtk/main/NEWS)). For an editor, that's worth a test on Hyprland and on a compositor without text-input-v3.

## Launcher and process model: wait by default, and fork only before the toolkit exists

### Prior art splits three ways, and per-process waiting avoids the classic bugs

Editors handle the launcher three ways. Some self-fork as one binary (gVim, Neovide, kitty). Some run a thin client against a resident server (Zed, VS Code, footclient, emacsclient). Some use a separate launcher that execs a GUI binary (WezTerm) ([vim gui_x11.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vim/vim/master/runtime/doc/gui_x11.txt); [zed cli main.rs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zed-industries/zed/main/crates/cli/src/main.rs); [wezterm main.rs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wezterm/wezterm/main/wezterm/src/main.rs)). The client/server designs have to invent a "window closed, here's the exit code" message, and VS Code shows how that goes wrong. Closing one pane of a split returns `--wait` early ([vscode #170444](https://github.com/microsoft/vscode/issues/170444)), and `--wait` returns no meaningful exit code, which once led SourceTree to delete the merge files ([vscode #157961](https://github.com/microsoft/vscode/issues/157961)). foo_edit's launcher calling `waitpid` on its own child sidesteps this whole class of bug (inference). Waiting by default is now the consensus. Neovide reversed its fork-by-default in 0.13 because "programs that fork are the exception, not the rule" ([neovide PR #2512](https://github.com/neovide/neovide/pull/2512)), and the familiar git failure, where a detaching editor makes git read an empty message, is well documented ([dev.to](https://dev.to/meg_gutshall/git-commit-aborts-before-i-can-create-a-commit-message-and-i-use---wait-1n8n)).

Neovide's detach path is fork, then setsid, then a full re-exec of itself, with exit status thrown away ([neovide src/main.rs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/neovide/neovide/main/src/main.rs)). That's exactly the "paid GTK loading twice" design the spike measured. kitty's C launcher is almost exactly the spike's untested idea. On Linux it redirects stdio, forks before Python, GLFW or GL load, and the child calls `setsid()` and simply carries on as kitty with no exec. On macOS it now execs, with a comment that fork without exec "used to work since in this case we havent yet loaded any major Cocoa libraries but in Tahoe 26.2 Apple broke that as well" ([kitty launcher main.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kovidgoyal/kitty/master/kitty/launcher/main.c)).

### Fork without exec is safe on Linux under one precondition

Colin Walters of GLib: "You basically can't do a fork() without exec() in a GLib program, and call GLib API in the child", because "calling fork() kills all other threads" ([GNOME bug 698606](https://bugzilla.gnome.org/show_bug.cgi?id=698606)). The hazard is threads. GLib's ELF constructor starts none on non-Windows, and its worker thread is created lazily ([glib-init.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/glib/main/glib/glib-init.c); [gmain.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/glib/main/glib/gmain.c)). The researchers infer that a fork taken while the process is single-threaded, before any GLib/GTK call, produces a child indistinguishable from a freshly started process. kitty's years in production support that, but no GNOME statement explicitly blesses it. None of the ~113 dependencies' constructors were audited, so the researchers recommend asserting `Threads: 1` from `/proc/self/status` at the top of `main` and falling back to fork+exec if it fails. On macOS, Apple engineers state that CoreFoundation "cannot be used on the child-side of fork(). If you fork(), you must follow that with an exec*() call", and that includes `daemon()` ([darwin-dev 2009](https://lists.apple.com/archives/darwin-dev/2009/May/msg00019.html)). Together with kitty's Tahoe 26.2 note, fork+exec on macOS is mandatory, not a preference.

There's an honest latency accounting the spike's framing skips. If one ELF links GTK, the dynamic loader maps all ~113 libraries **before `main`**. Fork without exec then pays that load once, the same as the two-binary design, but **the launcher itself is no longer 2.8 ms to main**. That matters for `--help`, `--version`, error paths, and running as `$EDITOR` without a display (inference). A busybox-style multi-call binary doesn't help either, because every personality still loads GTK. dlopen-deferring GTK would mean building the GUI as a `cdylib`, which is just a second artefact with extra rpath trouble (inference).

### Signals, detaching and exit codes

kitty issue #8680 is the race the spike's `-b` mode will hit. About 1 in 30 runs under a closing pty died because SIGHUP reached the child before it called `setsid()`. The fix was to make the parent block on a pipe until the child has run setsid. Kovid rejected double forking: "forking is expensive, there's no actual need for a double fork here" ([kitty issue #8680](https://github.com/kovidgoyal/kitty/issues/8680)). `POSIX_SPAWN_SETSID` exists in glibc 2.26 and later ([posix_spawn(3)](https://manpath.be/f37/3/posix_spawn); [glibc test](https://codebrowser.dev/glibc/glibc/posix/tst-posix_spawn-setsid.c.html)) and in the current macOS SDK (SDK header: sys/spawn.h:45-62). Rust's `Command` exposes `process_group` but not setsid, and `pre_exec` turns off the posix_spawn fast path ([Rust CommandExt](https://doc.rust-lang.org/std/os/unix/process/trait.CommandExt.html)). footclient passes through its child's exit code and reserves 220 and 230 for its own failures ([footclient.1](https://codeberg.org/dnkl/foot/raw/branch/master/doc/footclient.1.scd)).

The researchers recommend a launcher recipe built from these sources (the composition is theirs). Block SIGINT, SIGQUIT, SIGTSTP, SIGHUP and SIGTERM before fork. In the child, call `setsid()` first, then restore default handlers. Redirect the child's stdio away from the terminal, because `eprintln!` panics on EIO once the terminal closes. Pass through `WEXITSTATUS`, map signals to `128+n` with a one-line crash message, and reserve one code for "couldn't spawn". Never use `PR_SET_PDEATHSIG`, because the window has to outlive the launcher. Don't make detaching depend silently on `isatty`: a Neovide user found that redirecting output defeated the tty check ([neovide PR #2512](https://github.com/neovide/neovide/pull/2512)).

## Fonts and highlighting: lazy loading is the established pattern

### The tofu has a precise root cause

cosmic-text 0.19's fallback order is: requested family, then static per-script families, then a static common list, and finally **every remaining face already in the fontdb**. Each step looks up by name among faces already loaded and never asks the OS ([cosmic-text fallback/mod.rs](https://github.com/pop-os/cosmic-text/blob/main/src/font/fallback/mod.rs)). The macOS common list is `.SF NS`, Menlo, Apple Color Emoji, Geneva and Arial Unicode MS ([fallback/macos.rs](https://github.com/pop-os/cosmic-text/blob/main/src/font/fallback/macos.rs)). Apple Symbols isn't in it, and in the researchers' local Core Text probe `CTFontCreateForString(SF Mono, "U+2610")` returned Apple Symbols. So with only SF Pro and SF Mono loaded there's nothing to fall back to. The researchers infer that with the full scan, fallback "worked" only because the brute-force pass eventually stumbled on a covering face, and that this pass is what drove the slower first layout. In other words, the full scan was never good fallback, just exhaustive fallback. cosmic-text's own docs warn that `FontSystem::new()` "can take up to a second" in release builds ([system.rs](https://github.com/pop-os/cosmic-text/blob/main/src/font/system.rs)).

The fix the researchers propose builds on public escape hatches. Use `new_with_locale_and_db_and_fallback` with a trimmed db and a custom `Fallback` impl ([system.rs](https://github.com/pop-os/cosmic-text/blob/main/src/font/system.rs)). Preload a small platform set by path (Apple Color Emoji, Apple Symbols, Menlo on macOS; fontconfig's emoji and symbol matches on Linux). On a missed codepoint, ask the OS off-thread (`CTFontCreateForString` and then `kCTFontURLAttribute`; or fontconfig charset plus `FcFontMatch` and then `FC_FILE`), load just that file, and record the codepoint-to-path mapping in the cache. In the local probe, the first `CTFontCreateForString` cost about 13.7 ms and later calls 0.01 to 0.4 ms, so warming Core Text after first paint keeps that off the critical path. These were single runs, so treat them as indicative.

### The spike's path cache matches where upstream is heading

cosmic-text `main` (unreleased after 0.19.0) added a persistent font cache in June 2026, described as "a pure-Rust, dependency-free reimplementation of what fontconfig's `fc-cache` does". It's keyed on per-directory and per-file mtime plus size and is rewritten atomically ([cosmic-text cache.rs](https://github.com/pop-os/cosmic-text/blob/main/src/font/cache.rs)). It loads through fontdb's `push_face_info`, so the same pattern works against fontdb 0.23 today. The researchers add that a trimmed db stays faster than any full index, because construction still iterates every face, and that the invalidation key should include fontconfig config-file mtimes and the user's configured families. Why in-process fontconfig costs ~33 ms is unsourced. The researchers' unverified explanation is that config XML is parsed on every init and per-directory caches are mmapped, and `FC_DEBUG` with `strace -c` would confirm it. fontique, parley's font layer, is lazy on Linux (family names only, per-script `FcFontMatch` fallback) but **eagerly scans every font file on macOS** at `SystemFonts::new()` ([fontique coretext.rs](https://github.com/linebender/parley/blob/main/fontique/src/backend/coretext.rs); [fontconfig.rs](https://github.com/linebender/parley/blob/main/fontique/src/backend/fontconfig.rs)). So switching to parley wouldn't remove the macOS scan cost the spike measured.

### Query compilation is a known one-off cost; defer, cache, trim

`HighlightConfiguration::new` compiles highlights, injections and locals into one `Query`. Empty injections and locals switch those features off, and the configured struct is immutable and shareable ([docs.rs HighlightConfiguration](https://docs.rs/tree-sitter-highlight/latest/tree_sitter_highlight/struct.HighlightConfiguration.html)). Emacs developers measured query creation at **10.4 ms of a 15.8 ms font-lock call** before switching to compile-once ([emacs-devel](https://yhetil.org/emacs-devel/87sfpekf6t.fsf@gmail.com/)). Helix keeps each language's queries in `OnceLock` fields compiled on first use ([helix-core syntax.rs](https://github.com/helix-editor/helix/blob/master/helix-core/src/syntax.rs)). Zed limits highlight cursors to a byte range ([Zed commit #52674](https://git.secluded.site/zed/commit/6cdf954e2ce0e1a6b83ce116b81258d3b512adf1)). A rewrite of the HTML queries reported roughly 100x better performance ([Review Board r/14521](https://reviews.reviewboard.org/r/14521/)). The researchers add two things. tree-sitter `Query` objects can't be serialised to disk through the public API, so lazy compilation on a worker thread is as good as it gets. And `Highlighter::highlight` reparses from scratch, so incremental editing means owning the `Parser`/`Tree`/`QueryCursor` loop, as Helix (via tree-house) and Zed do. For grammars, use `tree-sitter-md` (not the 2021 `tree-sitter-markdown`) and `tree-sitter-sequel` (not the abandoned `tree-sitter-sql`) ([crates.io md](https://crates.io/crates/tree-sitter-md); [crates.io sequel](https://crates.io/crates/tree-sitter-sequel)). Markdown needs injections from day one, because block and inline are separate grammars (same source). Binary size for ten grammars hasn't been measured.

## Input latency: hand-off and glass are different numbers and both matter

### End-to-end numbers for good editors cluster at 30 to 36 ms at 60 Hz

Hardware testers (a microcontroller that sends a USB key and reads a photodiode) put the best macOS apps at 60 Hz at **Sublime 32.5 ms, TextEdit 33.4, Terminal.app 35.8 and kitty 36.1** on average. Hume also measured about 16 ms between the top and bottom of the screen from row scan-out ([Hume 2020](https://thume.ca/2020/05/20/making-a-latency-tester/)). Software tools such as Typometer leave out the keyboard, compositor scan-out and panel. kitty's developers quoted 35 ms with a hardware tester versus 7 ms with Typometer for the same terminal ([anarc.at](https://anarc.at/blog/2022-09-19-wayland-terminal-emulators/)), and blog figures for Ghostty and Zed latency mix the two kinds and should be ignored ([Vantaige](https://vantaige.io/blog/zed-1-vs-cursor-vs-vscode-real-benchmark-2026)). VTE's big 2024 win came from replacing a 40 Hz timer with per-frame drawing synced to the GTK frame clock, which brought GNOME 46 terminals "pretty much on par with Alacritty" ([bxt.rs](https://bxt.rs/blog/just-how-much-faster-are-the-gnome-46-terminals/)). Fatin estimated 26 ms of the budget sits outside the app, suggested 1 to 5 ms for the editor's own painting, and called 16 ms "quite arbitrary" ([Fatin](https://pavelfatin.com/typing-with-pleasure/)).

### A 16 ms glass target is impossible; the spike's redefinition is right

The researchers model it like this. A key at a random phase on a 60 Hz vsynced compositor waits uniformly from 0 to one frame for the next repaint, then at least one more frame to reach `presented`. That gives a commit-to-glass time of about uniform [T, 2T] for an early-compositing compositor: about 25 ms mean and 32 ms p95, before any app time. Even an ideal late-latching compositor leaves a p95 random-phase wait of about 0.95 T, or 15.8 ms. So **"key to glass p95 ≤16 ms at 60 Hz" can't be met by any composited client**. The spike's on-glass p95 of 40 ms fits this model plus a few ms. The model is the researchers' derivation. Its inputs are sourced: Sway's `max_render_time` semantics ([sway-output(5)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/swaywm/sway/master/sway/sway-output.5.scd)) and wp_presentation's definition of `presented` as when the update "turned into light the first time" ([wayland.app presentation-time](https://wayland.app/protocols/presentation-time)).

The model also explains the spike's most puzzling result: GTK hand-off at 14 ms against raw Wayland at 5 ms, with identical glass times. GTK waits for the frame callback *inside the app*. Raw Wayland commits at once and its buffer waits *inside the compositor*. The total comes out the same. So hand-off measured on its own can mislead in both directions, and needs to be reported next to glass and with frame-callback waiting separated out (inference). Compositor-side triple buffering (Mutter 48, Hyprland 0.50 `new_render_scheduling`) is described as adding no latency when frames are on time ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/GNOME-48-Triple-Buffering); [Hyprland 0.50](https://hypr.land/news/update50/)). fifo-v1 and commit-timing-v1 help with pacing, not keystroke latency ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/Wayland-Protocols-1.38)). There's no credible ProMotion keystroke-to-photon data, and the macOS 4.4 ms is a hand-off figure, not comparable with Wayland's 40 ms glass.

### Measurement method

Credible measurement needs a stated start and end point on a single clock. wp_presentation's clock is announced at bind and "applies only to presentation timestamps, not to core input event timestamps" ([wayland.app presentation-time](https://wayland.app/protocols/presentation-time)). Its flags (`vsync`, `hw_clock`, `hw_completion`, `zero_copy`, `discarded`) show how far each sample can be trusted (same source). Hume added random jitter between samples so results wouldn't lock to the refresh, and Dan Luu used 10k presses per condition and showed that tails blow out under load ([Hume](https://thume.ca/2020/05/20/making-a-latency-tester/); [Dan Luu](https://danluu.com/term-latency/)). The researchers recommend injecting keys through uinput with a `CLOCK_MONOTONIC` send time, because wtype goes through the compositor and `wl_keyboard.key` time has an undefined base. That clock reasoning is the researchers' general knowledge, not fetched. They also recommend at least 500 samples per condition with randomised 100 to 250 ms gaps, a same-session baseline of foot plus neovim, and a Teensy-style photodiode rig for occasional calibration.

## Crates and packaging: churn is the real cost, and one name needs an xtask

### Two speeds of crate

As of 3 November 2026, the stack falls into two groups ([crates.io API](https://crates.io/api/v1/crates/cosmic-text) per crate). The slow, stable ones are tiny-skia 0.12.0 (after a two-year gap and a move to linebender, with a README that still calls it "more of a research project"), objc2 0.6.x with framework crates at 0.3.x since January 2025, image 0.25.x, and yeslogic-fontconfig-sys. The fast, breaking ones are cosmic-text (0.16 to 0.19 within four months), parley (six minors in eleven months), vello_cpu (0.1, 0.2 and 0.3 in ten weeks), tree-sitter 0.27, and gtk4 0.11 with **MSRV 1.92** ([crates.io gtk4](https://crates.io/crates/gtk4); [linebender/tiny-skia](https://github.com/linebender/tiny-skia)). cosmic-text has landed behaviour changes in patch releases, including an MSRV bump in 0.17.1 and changed motion semantics in 0.17.2, so pinning alone doesn't protect you and every bump needs a changelog read ([cosmic-text CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pop-os/cosmic-text/main/CHANGELOG.md)). Its contributor graph is dominated by one System76 engineer (477 commits against 61 for the next contributor), while parley's is spread across at least three people ([GitHub API cosmic-text](https://api.github.com/repos/pop-os/cosmic-text/contributors); [parley](https://api.github.com/repos/linebender/parley/contributors)). swash 0.2.10 pins skrifa ≤0.44 while current skrifa is 0.49, so mixing it with newer fontations crates may pull in two skrifa versions ([swash deps](https://crates.io/api/v1/crates/swash/0.2.10/dependencies)). docs.rs marks `cocoa` as deprecated in favour of objc2, and baseview migrated to objc2 in May 2026 ([docs.rs cocoa](https://docs.rs/cocoa/latest); [baseview PR #240](https://github.com/RustAudio/baseview/pull/240)).

### Packaging norms accept two binaries; Flatpak doesn't suit $EDITOR

Debian's Rust policy puts all bin targets of a crate into one binary package ([Debian Rust Team policy](https://rust-team.pages.debian.net/book/policy.html)). Arch names multi-binary packages after the crate and documents `cargo fetch --locked` / `cargo build --frozen` builds ([Arch Rust package guidelines](https://manual.archlinux.page/package-guidelines/rust/)). Fedora forbids bundled crates unless there's no other way to build ([rust2rpm #139](https://pagure.io/fedora-rust/rust2rpm/issue/139)). With MSRV 1.92 and fast-moving dependencies, the researchers judge official archive inclusion impractical for now, and recommend project-built .deb and .rpm files via cargo-deb 3.8.0 and cargo-generate-rpm 0.21.1 (both active: [cargo-deb](https://crates.io/crates/cargo-deb); [cargo-generate-rpm](https://crates.io/crates/cargo-generate-rpm)). On macOS, Zed signs with `codesign`, notarises with `notarytool submit --wait` and staples ([zed bundle-mac](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zed-industries/zed/main/script/bundle-mac)), and WezTerm `lipo`s a universal binary and signs with the hardened runtime ([wezterm ci/deploy.sh](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wezterm/wezterm/main/ci/deploy.sh)). rcodesign hasn't had a crate release in about two years ([apple-codesign](https://crates.io/crates/apple-codesign)). Flatpak is a poor fit for an `$EDITOR` tool. `flatpak-spawn --host` needs an extra D-Bus permission ([flatpak-spawn(1)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man1/flatpak-spawn.1.html)), and FileZilla's Flatpak broke external editors ([flathub FileZilla #40](https://github.com/flathub/org.filezillaproject.Filezilla/issues/40)).

### One name definition: fixed Cargo bin names plus a templating xtask

Cargo can't compute a `[[bin]] name`, and the issue asking for a configurable output name is closed ([cargo #1706](https://github.com/rust-lang/cargo/issues/1706)). Prior art handles names badly. Zed `sed`s per-channel `package.metadata.bundle-*` tables into Cargo.toml before running a forked cargo-bundle. Alacritty keeps names in Makefile variables. WezTerm hard-codes them across scripts ([zed bundle-mac](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zed-industries/zed/main/script/bundle-mac); [alacritty Makefile](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/alacritty/alacritty/master/Makefile); [wezterm deploy.sh](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wezterm/wezterm/main/ci/deploy.sh)). Zed's workaround shows why bundlers that read static `[package.metadata]` fight a single-definition rule. The researchers recommend this pattern, which is their own synthesis:

- Put a single `[workspace.metadata.app]` (slug, display name, reverse DNS) in the root Cargo.toml.
- Have a shared `build.rs` emit `cargo:rustc-env` values, which code reads through `env!` for titles, `--version`, clap names and config paths.
- Keep Cargo bin names neutral and fixed (`launcher`, `window`).
- Have an xtask copy and rename the binaries and render Info.plist, `.desktop`, AppStream, the man page, PKGBUILD, the spec file and cargo-deb metadata from templates.
- Run a CI job that changes the slug and greps the built artefacts for the old name.

## Open decisions and the evidence for each option

### Single binary versus two binaries

**One binary, fork without exec on Linux, fork+exec on macOS.** For it: kitty has shipped exactly this on Linux for years ([kitty main.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kovidgoyal/kitty/master/kitty/launcher/main.c)); GTK is loaded once; there's one artefact and one name. Against it: the launcher pays the full ~50 ms library load before `main` even for `--help`, errors and display-less use; safety depends on an unaudited precondition (no constructor among ~113 libraries starts a thread), which can only be checked at runtime; GLib's maintainers warn off the general pattern ([GNOME 698606](https://bugzilla.gnome.org/show_bug.cgi?id=698606)); and on macOS the exec costs a second framework load, so the single-binary benefit there is purely about packaging. **Two binaries (libc-only launcher plus window binary).** For it: 2.8 ms to `main`; no fork-safety reasoning at all; WezTerm and Zed ship this way; Debian, Arch and macOS bundle layout all accept it ([Debian policy](https://rust-team.pages.debian.net/book/policy.html); [Arch](https://manual.archlinux.page/package-guidelines/rust/)). Against it: two names to derive (the xtask handles that), and the two have to stay version-locked, for example by exchanging a protocol version. The researchers judge both sound and two binaries lower-risk. This synthesis agrees, and adds that time to window is about the same either way, so the choice comes down to risk and launcher responsiveness, not keystroke or cold-start goals. The "single binary if possible" goal is met in spirit by a single *package* with one user-facing name.

### How to define the keystroke threshold

**To hand-off only.** This rewards nothing the user perceives: GTK's 14 ms and raw Wayland's 5 ms produce identical glass. **On glass, in milliseconds.** At p95 ≤16 ms at 60 Hz it's unattainable, because the random-phase wait alone is about 15.8 ms (inference from the vsync model). **Refresh-relative.** This is robust across 60, 120 and 144 Hz panels. The researchers propose:

- **Hand-off:** p95 ≤8 ms and p99 ≤16 ms, with frame-callback waiting measured separately so GTK isn't charged for compositor pacing.
- **Glass:** p50 ≤1.75 and p95 ≤2.5 refresh periods (about 29 and 42 ms at 60 Hz). The spike's 40 ms passes at the edge.
- **Baseline:** no worse than foot plus neovim on the same session by more than 0.25 refresh.

There's no published industry standard for refresh-period targets, so these numbers are proposals and not sourced thresholds. A gap to flag: if hand-off is gated at 8 ms p95 *without* separating out the frame-callback wait, GTK's measured 14 ms fails, which would be measuring the toolkit's pacing rather than foo_edit's work.

### GSK renderer choice

**Default (Vulkan on Wayland), 234 ms.** It's upstream's choice since 4.16 and gets the most testing, but it's the slowest start, and Ghostty reports GL/Vulkan conflicts ([GTK NEWS](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GNOME/gtk/main/NEWS); [Ghostty](https://github.com/ghostty-org/ghostty/blob/main/src/apprt/gtk/class/application.zig)). **GL via `GDK_DISABLE=vulkan`, 213 ms.** This is Ghostty's production choice, it's already X11's default, and both GPU renderers upload memory textures the same way, so the CPU-rendered content gives nothing up. **Cairo, 147 ms.** Fastest cold start, but it's the documented fallback, composites on the CPU, loses offload, and its keystroke p95 at 4K x2 hasn't been measured. The evidence favours GL as the default. Cairo should stay open only as an opt-in until the spike measures its hand-off and glass percentiles under the same rig. If cairo matches GL on keystroke latency, the 66 ms start-up gain is big enough relative to the 250 ms budget to reconsider.

### cosmic-text versus parley

**Stay on cosmic-text.** For it: the spike already runs on it; it ships `Editor` and buffer abstractions; Zed's Linux text system uses it ([Zed cosmic_text_system.rs](https://github.com/zed-industries/zed/blob/main/crates/gpui_wgpu/src/cosmic_text_system.rs)); the tofu and scan costs can be fixed through its public `Fallback` and db hooks; upstream is adding start-up features such as the persistent cache. Against it: effectively one maintainer; breaking changes in patch releases; about ten harfrust minors behind current ([CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pop-os/cosmic-text/main/CHANGELOG.md); [harfrust](https://crates.io/crates/harfrust)). **Move to parley.** For it: a broader maintainer base; Bevy migrated to it ([bevy PR #22879](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/pull/22879)); one project's ADR benchmark found parley about 4.8x faster on mixed lines and cosmic-text "vastly slower" at cold start ([pragmatrix/massive #154](https://github.com/pragmatrix/massive/pull/154)), though that's one machine and one font; richer span layout suits a markdown editor with headings and inline code. Against it: just as much churn; fontique scans every font file eagerly on macOS, so it doesn't fix the measured macOS cost ([fontique coretext.rs](https://github.com/linebender/parley/blob/main/fontique/src/backend/coretext.rs)); parley's own editing helpers are still being built out ([flui #1403](https://github.com/vanyastaff/flui/pull/1403)). The two research notes lean different ways: the fonts researcher says stay, the crates researcher says parley long-term. The common ground, and this synthesis's recommendation, is to stay on cosmic-text behind a thin internal layout trait and run a head-to-head layout and cold-start benchmark in the spike harness, since no independent benchmark covers a 1,000-line buffer.

### Minimum GTK version

The relevant facts: `GdkMemoryTextureBuilder` with update regions arrived in 4.16 ([docs.gtk.org](https://docs.gtk.org/gdk4/class.MemoryTextureBuilder.html)); plain `GdkMemoryTexture` works back to 4.0; the Wayland Vulkan default and the frame-clock rework landed in 4.16 and 4.23/4.24. Distro versions are Ubuntu 24.04 LTS 4.14, Debian 12 4.8, Debian 13 4.18, Ubuntu 26.04 LTS 4.22, Fedora 43 4.20, and Arch and Tumbleweed 4.24 ([Repology gtk](https://repology.org/project/gtk/versions)). gtk4-rs 0.11 builds against 4.0 by default, with `v4_N` features raising the floor ([gtk4-sys 0.11.3](https://crates.io/crates/gtk4-sys/0.11.3)). **A 4.14 floor** keeps Ubuntu 24.04, with the 4.16 builder path gated at runtime. Because the builder only narrows damage and tiling delivers most of the win without it, the cost is small. **A 4.16 floor** drops Ubuntu 24.04 unless those users get Flatpak, and Flatpak is a poor fit for `$EDITOR` use, which this synthesis treats as a real conflict in the gtk4_linux note's suggestion. **A 4.18 floor** matches Debian 13 and every current non-LTS release, and gets the post-4.17 renderer clean-up. Remember that latency numbers measured on 4.24's reworked frame clock may not hold on older versions. The researchers recommend `v4_14` with runtime gating. Whatever floor is chosen, gtk4 0.11's MSRV of 1.92 means building with rustup toolchains rather than distro compilers, which is a bigger practical constraint than the GTK floor itself.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the picture in one important way. The spike's numbers mostly measure *where waiting happens*, not how much work is done. IOSurface versus CGImage, strips versus whole-window cairo, and GTK versus raw Wayland on glass all come down to removing a fixed hand-off or moving a vsync wait between the app and the compositor. That makes the latency spec, not the renderer, the decision that most needs getting right. A spec written in hand-off milliseconds alone would have pushed the owner towards raw Wayland for no perceptible gain, and one written as 16 ms on glass would have been impossible from day one.

The second insight is structural. Nearly every fast GTK app in the prior art gets its speed from staying resident, and foo_edit's one-process-per-file model deliberately gives that up in exchange for exact `waitpid` semantics that sidestep a whole class of VS Code-style bugs. That's a good trade, but it means the cold-start budget has no slack to hide behind. The remaining cold-start wins sit in the renderer choice, library count, fonts and the launcher layout, and the two-binary layout is the one option that improves launcher responsiveness without adding safety reasoning. The next round of the spike should measure cairo's keystroke percentiles, a cosmic-text versus parley head-to-head, a Sysprof breakdown of `gtk_init`, and buffer-age-2 damage correctness on macOS. Those four measurements would close every remaining open decision.
